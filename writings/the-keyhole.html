The Keyhole

A surreal fable about pain, fate, and the search for a suffering that feels meaningful.

Nevertheless, ladies and gentlemen, my journey continues here, ever going, never stopping, always forward. The automated sliding glass doors open with a soft whir, giving way to the stale, over-conditioned air that sucks me in like I’m an unwanted piece of debris underneath a high-powered industrial vacuum.

The stucco-adorned lobby greets me with its fake, optical-illusion-like brick floors and weathered copper pipes that snake above, emitting a subtle trickle of sewage or cooled water or some other strange mix of unknown liquid pulsing through like blood in veins. I arrive at the metallic-black front desk reminiscent of my days spent in the cubicle on 56th, at which an old woman with frazzled white hair plays a computer game. Every time she clicks the creaky, outdated mouse, her character jumps one line further, moving into the next square of the scary, trafficked street ahead. The lady’s focus is absolute—there doesn’t seem to be an end, just line after line of cars and trucks and buses whizzing by.

What pleasure does she get out of such a game? Is it the cars, trucks, trains, bikes, all making their journey off the screen and to a destination never disclosed, or is it the character itself, trudging ever forward, finding purchase on each new street, unaware of what could collide with her next, relying completely and solely on the shaking index finger that ever so slightly twitches?

I clear my throat.

She pauses the game and slowly turns her head, facing me with blank, languid eyes that travel from the messy, dark-haired mop on my head downward, observing the spaghetti-sauce-stained Cubs T-shirt and the holes in my New Balance 550s. Then again, maybe she’s just assessing basic threat levels—affirming I am, in fact, not an ultra-nationalist terrorist or an irate ex.

“Name and name of who you are here to see,” she croaks.

“Odysseus. Call me Od. I’m here for the show upstairs.”

She blinks twice before giving a wide, dentured grin that reminds me of the skull-and-bones logo imprinted on the flag of any self-respecting pirate, and slides me a key stamped with the Latinate title iter. She points at the staircase to the left.

“You can’t miss it.”

“Thanks.”

The lady swivels back to her screen, her face returning to a placid expression of terminal boredom, before flatly but cryptically telling me to “enjoy the ride.”

Does she expect me not to? The tone of her voice seemed to hint at something I’m missing—a trick or trial or task—some hidden step I won’t just see and avoid, but discover and slam into head-on.

No matter.

The key is here, the stairs there.

I’m off.

Up the smoke-yellow steps, the fourth squeaking as my right foot presses down. The railing has gum stains and a film of grime on it; still, I hold on anyway. I’ll clutch the gross, uncleaned pole for dear life if it’ll keep me from falling backward and ending the journey too soon.

After what feels like an hour of climbing—huffing and puffing my way up each rubber-studded step—a fluorescent-pink cargo ship icon appears above an otherwise unmarked doorway. Once my breath returns, I push the heavy metal door inward, revealing a dark hallway dimly lit only by the keyholes located on the smooth mahogany doors that stretch past the visible and into an unseeable beyond.

This kind of unending vermiform pathway unsettles me. Who knows what lies beyond or which door will open? Too many options. Too little space. The hallway feels like a shaft, a tube that can close in and squeeze at any given moment.

It reminds me of the pale-green corridors at the hospital—every door locked, each filled with screams and splats and knocks and begs. These corridors would never end. I’d take my mandatory meds and stroll them with a Styrofoam cup of coffee curing the morning fog. Every turn I took would be ninety degrees to the right. This was my only way of knowing where I’d been and how long I’d walked, since, besides the sounds, everything looked and felt the same. Once the third turn had been made, I knew I was back where I started.

At the base of the first door on the right, I kneel down and put my eye to the keyhole. It blinds me and makes phosphenes bloom in my vision, like when I was six years old on the swing and a friend told me that if I stared at the sun for more than five seconds, I’d take some of the light with me, allowing me to see in the dark.

Obviously, that did not go as planned.

I frown and study the brass opening.

Keyholes—especially locked ones—have always fascinated me. By using an indiscreet piece of metal, one can unlock an entirely new world. A place of sojourn, a domicile, a secret. And there can be as many or as few tools of opening it as one pleases. The only way for the non-owners to get a peek at the inside is to put their eyes or their—

Ahh.

I lean down again and lower my ear to the glow.

“Jon, look! Look at him! He’s walking!”

He rushes in and takes a quick picture; the camera’s flash reflects in the child’s wide eyes. The baby giggles, waddling this way and that, arms outstretched to his waiting mother. She’s sitting in a plush blue chair in front of the television with her arms opened wide in greeting.

As the baby comes within a few feet of her, he sharply turns, beelining toward the TV blaring a kiddy song about eating your veggies, and trips forward, hitting his head on the mantle with a loud crack.

Jon rushes forward, scooping the baby up with a flourish. His wife screams bloody murder and stumbles over her feet getting up from the chair, almost kicking their silver Persian cat across the room in the process. The baby isn’t crying. He lets out a soft, sedated gurgle and stares blankly at the screen. Only when the parents rush him into the kitchen to clean the bloody red mark on his forehead does he start to wail.

The key in my pocket burns white-hot, forcing a leap away from the door.

This isn’t it.

Not what I’m looking for.

Because what’s the operating principle here? That the suffering on display must be agented? That it must be the direct, traceable result of a conscious choice—one that leads him away from his oedipal origins and toward a new, more comforting beacon of warmth?

Surely Freud would have much to say here. He would describe this diverted pathing as solely an infantile response to the mother’s lack of mammary-gland generosity—a sub-instinctual response to the greater source of guiding and care.

For me, this is lunacy.

A farce.

Even if it’s not, it is of no concern of mine. I am not an analyst. I am just a man.

For me, it’s a simple tragedy of gross motor skills and Newtonian physics. An insufficient horror, an unaesthetic pain.

The horror here—for lack of a better term—is void of narrative. Of drive. Of motion. It is the universe’s own brand of banal, clerical cruelty, an incident report filed in the great, bland bureaucracy of senseless accidents.

And what I’m here for—what this whole grim pilgrimage feels like it’s for—is something with a signature.

An authored pain.

So I turn away.

The keyhole fades to black with a faint, dry click—the same sound as the old woman’s mouse. I wonder for a second whether it’s a coincidental similarity or if, just beneath my feet, she’s moved on to the next line of traffic.

After shaking the miasmic fog out of my head, I continue forward. Each door emits faint buzzes, human laughs, and cheers. The sounds purl around the room, turning the hallway into a condensing and contracting worm of reverberations.

Turning right again, I choose a door out of pure aleatory chance, throwing the proverbial dice, and crouch.

“I’m not doing this with you again, Stella. You can’t fucking sit here silently and not tell me what’s wrong. I’m not some omniscient God, calling the shots before you. I’m a living, breathing human with my own worries and struggles. Have some sonder.”

Stella sits on the fuzzy navy-blue couch, curly blonde hair drooping over her eyes. She wrings her hands together, clenching and unclenching nervously.

“Nothing’s wrong, Sam. Why do you think there is?”

“You sit here, won’t engage, and give these fucking soft little sniffs like the world’s gonna end if I don’t bow down and supplicate to some unknown deep desire you refuse to even hint at.”

She sniffs.

“I’m not mad. Just let me watch my show in peace.”

Behind Sam, season four, episode ten of Friends flashes back after a Cheez-It commercial.

Sam stares at Stella, blinking, his eyes flashing with lambent rage. When she continues to stare blankly through him, he storms away, slamming their door so hard it creates a lightning-bolt-shaped fissure in the base. He takes the trumpet vase of daffodils off their dresser, pours the water and flowers onto the ground, and hurls the porcelain to the floor, screaming in anger, then shock, when he sees a shard sticking through his New Balance sneaker and out the other side.

Now this—this is that juicy, mellifluous content I came here for.

The sharp pain in my pocket feels good.

Deserved.

It’s the stuff that cleanses, the hydrogen peroxide, the electroshock therapy, the pink bar of Dove body soap. It improves. Opens the wound and makes you stitch it up yourself again.

As I get up, I notice a similar-looking hole in my shoes.

A place of entry.

Lifting the shoe up, it is clear something went out the other side.

A surge of something that isn’t energy—more like a pure, clarifying current—catapults me to my feet. I’m running, driven forward by the radiant warmth of the key jostling in my pocket.

If it keeps heating up like this, a hole’s gonna burn through.

I trek forward, my footsteps and the ambient noise of the doors echoing around me. It creates this chaotic feedback loop of sounds in my head, where I’m not sure what’s coming from the outside versus the inside.

In college, my fraternity brothers and I had a fantasy football league where the winner took home a whopping two thousand dollars pooled by the mandatory buy-in and a glorious silver trophy specially ordered to say: MASTER BALL HANDLER.

It was a big deal.

We all took it with the utmost sincerity—Excel spreadsheets, podcasts, draft scanning.

Though everyone said they just wanted the glory, the truth was much more sinister. The loser had to take the four-hour drive to Minneapolis with all the brothers and go to Orfield Laboratories. In this lab, they have an anechoic chamber which happens to be the quietest room in the world. The loser would have to sit in this room alone, with all the brothers watching through a one-way mirror, until two full days were up or they had to be pulled out.

Now, this sounds like a relatively innocuous punishment.

Some would even call this “fun.”

But we knew.

We’d seen the look on their faces after fifteen minutes.

Anyway, that year I had lost and was, to say the least, despondent. Everyone was laughing and throwing empty beer cans at me in the car—I sat quietly along. The president, one of the only nicer individuals in this whole ordeal, slid me two bars of Xanax and told me to take them fifteen minutes before.

“It’ll silence the demons. Trust me, bro. You’ll need them.”

After taking the pills and shaking the hand of a grinning scientist who had just been given five hundred big ones to let me stay in there, I entered.

Within the first ten seconds, I started to hear my stomach sloshing. It was like a laundry machine spinning over and over, washing only the remnants of a blue Voltage Mountain Dew, some stale saltines, and two little chill pills.

Soon after, my heart started reverberating around the room—now, I know this wasn’t possible considering the whole point of an anechoic chamber is to absorb sound, but nevertheless that heart beat.

Then my thoughts started to echo.

Girls, classes, my friends, the room, my family, secrets, love, death, hate—all purled around, echoing into a compendium of orchestral cries. I prayed it was all in my head, because if, through the fulgent mirror in front of me, my brothers could hear this, there would surely be a psychical lashing to come.

And then the bars hit.

The pills washed me into a deep cerebral calm. I listened to the sounds not with fear or anxiety, but with acceptance and appreciation.

My head became a personal resting ground.

This is how I feel right now, looking around, hearing all the noises and cries emitting from those small lambent keyholes.

The left might be the way.

Carefully, I inspect two adjacent doors, trying to parse which will reveal what I need. Though it may be my imagination, one hole looks just a smidge brighter. I move close to it and slide my ear downward.

“Haaaaapppy birthday toooo you! Happppy birrrrthday to youuu!”

At least twenty children crowd around a beaming, now seven-year-old girl, singing. The girl’s front baby tooth is missing, and she has bouncing curly blonde hair. Her mother flits back and forth, preparing gifts and party favors, as well as alcohol for the adults.

They’re in the backyard, the smell of petrichor and freshly mown grass permeating the faint sugar smell redolent in the vanilla icing.

After the last “Happpy birrrrrthday dear Stelllla, happppy birthday tooooo you,” she spittily blows out the candles, and a short, dark-haired boy in the back screams, “And maaannny more!”

The children glare at him and the parents look disconcertingly at each other.

Stella doesn’t notice—she’s entranced by the glow of the moment.

After the red velvet cake is devoured, they all crowd together, ready for Stella to open presents. The one she’s opening now, the boy can see, is his. He spent hours and hours wrapping it himself, cutting again and again, taping and retaping. His mother and father took him to the store earlier that day and gave him a budget of one hundred bucks to pick out her gift.

She opens it slowly, inhaling sharply as the gift comes to fruition.

An iPod!

Her eyes go wide, pupils swallowing the blue from them, and she rushes the boy to give him a big hug. She releases him, gives a big toothless grin, and runs back to the center of the circle.

Pulling away is easy here.

The scene does not encapsulate.

What use is it?

There’s no room for sterile happiness in my life. Surely, it’s not what I’m searching for.

At least the right made me feel.

Still, the image touches a familiar string inside me. Vaguely, there is a memory of someone with a similar shining grin and bouncing curls, but much older. The picture fluctuates, shrinking and engorging, ending with her hair no longer there and big bags under her eyes.

The remembrance floats around in my cerebrum for a second before dissipating.

And so the march continues.

I hug the right side, fingertips brushing against each door. When a finger gets caught on a hinge, I stop and bend down.

“Fuuuuuck that. Saying the Coen brothers are better than Tarantino is like saying plain Cheerios are better than Honey Nut. It’s just not a valid opinion, man,” says a college-aged, dark-haired man with stubble who looks like an older version of the boy from earlier.

His feet are perched on the coffee table and he’s taking a drag of a just-lit joint. Inhaling heavily, he makes a plosive and blows upward toward the ceiling.

Once the smoke dissipates, he passes it to his shaggy-haired, tatted friend, who says, “You just don’t get the history, maaaan. Lebowski is generational. I mean—fuck—‘the dude abides.’ It’s iconic. How many Tarantino quotes do you know?”

He chuckles.

“You’re out of your element, Donny.”

This elicits a crumpled Taki wrapper being thrown at his head.

“What’d we say about that one? Just because I’m a Donny doesn’t mean you can weaponize my favorite movie against me.”

In response, he carefully grabs the lightly smoking doobie from Donny and inhales until the cherry-red end is too close to his fingers to comfortably hold. Donny counts to ten, shouting louder with each number, before the man finally exhales, coughing aggressively into his elbow.

He drops the butt onto the carpet and goes pale white.

Though no one touched the remote, the volume on the TV—where commentators talk in slow, ramping voices as the ball gets closer to the net—gets louder.

Filters of blue, gray, and green flash.

Phosphenes bloom.

A vignette forms in the corners.

The man grabs his stomach, lurches forward, and vomits a lurid red paste, spraying the table, Donny, and the TV in one fell swoop.

I jerk back, falling on my ass, and blink for a few seconds.

The key in my pocket feels heavy instead of hot this time. It takes much more energy to stand up, and I swear the threads of my pocket are loosening.

This one—at least mentally—doesn’t make me feel much at all.

It’s just a simple sedation. A chemical change in the mind. A cluster of tetrahydrocannabinoids curated in dimly lit greenhouses with 24/7 water control and underpaid harvesters gathering product to make big money for the man to mess with a poor college kid’s oversensitive receptors.

I’d do some now if I could.

It’d help the worries.

I must be careful now.

More frugal.

There’s this strange sensation that if the key were to fall, it’d fall through my pants and disappear into the thick Turkish carpet beneath me. Then I’d have to put my tail between my legs and slink back out from whence I came. I’d have to tell the lady I lost her key, and she’d pause her little game and grimace at me—point to the door outside and tell me never to return.

Worse, though:

What if she wasn’t there?

What if she died when a car hit her in the silly computer game?

Or disappeared underground looking for the missing key?

I’d have not only failed but would be alone in my failure—not even a cranky old lady to be upset with me.

And so I must find a door to open.

A keyhole to jam into and turn, for keys don’t last forever.

They wear and tear and fall through pockets.

They become brittle and chip away.

I huff and look around.

The end of the corridor—if there is one—is nowhere in sight.

Six steps forward, then.

A look right, then left—no.

Back to right.

Surely that one is right.

Commercial pop blares out of the Cinemark’s massive lobby speakers—the reactions to each song varying with age—all the children bobbing their heads, in the know, the adults talking about how superior their generation’s music was, and the workers grimacing and shooting looks at each other when “Firework” comes on for what feels like the seventy-fifth time this evening.

An inoffensive but expansive bar plays sports reruns on its three wide-screen plasma TVs. Only one barstool is occupied by an elderly man staring deeply into his undrunk Guinness, wondering how to split the G.

Booths line the opposite wall, with cracked red pleather seats and aluminum tables that wobble when weight shifts on top of them. In the booth closest to the revolving glass doors, the boy from the birthday party sits with his mother, brother, and sister and devours chicken tenders and fries, making sure to lather each bite with a healthy portion of ketchup.

They watch people walk up to the ticket counter, make a purchase, and saunter into the dimly lit hallway that holds an eclectic mix of foreign films, dramas, thrillers, romance, horror, and kiddie movies behind its wide arching doors. The family plays a game where they try to guess which movie each person is going to see based on appearance and gait.

Of course there are basic stereotypes—couples seeing romance, shady individuals into horror, children into the plethora of G- and PG-rated movies at the very back—but you’d be surprised how often people see something completely antithetical to their persona. The mother has a sheet of paper and catalogs each child’s guess so they can tally the winner at the end.

The family has just finished watching the third installment of the new Disney film Humpty-Dumpty. This one is the most extreme of the series, featuring Humpty with a tiny crack on his forehead, viscous yellow yolk dripping out, as he balances on a tightrope positioned forty floors above the New York metro.

During a particular dry spell where no one is buying tickets, the boy stops stuffing himself with chicken fingers and begins to argue with his mom about why he ought to be allowed to watch the R-rated shoot-em-up movie where a sniper holds his ground for forty-eight hours. He’s developed a fierce line of reasoning based on the salience of educating the youth on gun laws.

Suddenly, a crowd of forty kids, chaperoned by one tired-looking mother, walks in.

The boy stops his diatribe and stares.

The children chatter away—trading cards, showing one another videos, talking about the most vulgar movies they’ve watched when their parents weren’t supervising them.

A boy with a B-day Boy ballcap trailing closely behind his mother turns around and sees the booth. He tugs on his mother’s shirt and whispers something in her ear.

She purses her lips and turns to look at the family.

In the booth, the mom has figured out what is going on and is already putting the tally sheet into her purse and calling the waiter over for the check. The weary chaperone trudges over to the booth and gives a languid smile.

All the kids go quiet and stare at the boy.

“Hey, Sam! How are you?”

The boy holds back tears and croaks, “I’m okay, Ms. Morgan. Birthday party for Matt?”

She nods solemnly.

“We’re going to watch Humpty-Dumpty 3 if you’d like to join. I can buy an extra ticket if—”

At these words the boy completely breaks down, and the children murmur to one another, watching him fly out through the doors with his mom in close pursuit.

“FUCK!”

The door meets my foot over and over again.

This isn’t what I need.

Not what I came here for.

Where is it?

Where?

Kicking until my toe crumples, my leg goes numb. From inside my pocket, there are just two threads holding the key in place.

Tears form.

The doors continue to evade me—I’m just not sure how to find the right one.

All the doors are identical.

Why?

Why must I see this?

I drop down onto the carpet and run my fingers through the threads. It feels soft—like my childhood dog Calidus, a friendly St. Bernard that slept in my bed every night.

Carefully, I take off both shoes and socks and inspect the damage. The toe is for sure broken—it’s a mangled, unnaturally angled knot of red and purple. Touching it causes sparks to fly across my vision and a violent wave of nausea to form in my stomach.

Just for a second, I lay my head on the carpet and stare at the mold-speckled ceiling.

I swear it was cleaner at the beginning.

Everything always is, I guess.

Two threads.

Two goes left.

There’s no prediction machine for this.

One must simply move forward and hope their steps take them to the right place.

But maybe I walk a ways first.

Take a trek downward.

Take that loopty-loop in the ward with my eyes closed, never expecting to hit an end but always hoping to get closer.

And so I hobble on.

Left foot first, right foot dragging behind.

Time becomes nonexistent.

One step.

Another.

Another.

No thoughts appear—just a fuzzy, lambent static where my mind used to be.

The ceiling gets dirtier as the keyholes get brighter.

When my sweat is dripping onto the carpet, my toe is completely numb, and the key in my pocket has pulled the right side of my pants down to my knee, I collapse and crawl left.

The smell of sea salt wafts through open windows and intermingles with bacon and eggs being cooked on the stove by a kind-looking elderly woman with smile lines and curly hair. She flips a piece of bacon and watches the waves crash against the shore of the white-sand beach.

Pictures plaster the walls of the house, showing children and grandchildren and pets and old wedding photos. There is not a TV, phone, or electronic of any kind in sight.

An elderly man sits on the patio outside, reading an old Salinger novel. He chuckles at a line, which gives him a coughing fit and causes him to wheeze and clutch his chest. Once it subsides, he takes a sip of his decaf coffee and turns around to smile at his wife fussing wi—

I voluntarily pull out.

Cliché.

Banal.

Where’s the struggle?

This is not for me.

I refuse to insert the key here.

Nevertheless, I stare at it.

Something draws me—the warm slosh of the ocean, problems—at least the mental ones—set in the past.

The moment lasts for just a fleeting second before I snap out of it.

Settling is another form of suffering.

The next door will be better.

I feel it.

I know it.

There’s no energy left to walk further, no threads left to waste.

The door right there—the right one—will do.

I finger the single thread in my pocket, careful not to snap it.

The key feels different.

I pull it out.

It’s worn and stubby, the copper casing turned green with age. My fingers run against the grooves, feeling every pit and scarred edge.

There’s no previewing my fate here.

It’s unchangeable.

I rest the green, rusted key on my lips for a few moments. It tastes of blood and grime and tears and lost opportunity.

With trembling hands, I insert the key and turn the lock, pushing the creaking door open with my broken toe to feel that sting one last time.

It feels right.

When the pain pushes you forward, everything just fits into place.

One left step to the doorway and one right hobbled step inside.

I close my eyes, preparing for the worst and hoping for the best.

The door snaps shut behind me.

I open my eyes and blink.
