Introduction

The nature of reality is an unapologetically dynamic domain, humanity even more so - our semantics, morality, and culture change in every passing moment. The present is never like the past, and the future will never be like the present. Despite this glaring dynamism, the law attempts to stabilize humanity’s becoming. It boxes in what is and can be based on what was, and society goes along with it, oneirically consenting to the mechanisms governing our freedom and livelihood. The most egregious way law attempts to stabilize is through stare decisis: a foundational concept holding that courts and judges should honor precedent, or the rulings and opinions from prior cases (American Bar Association, 2022). As practiced in the U.S. legal system, stare decisis is a frequently harmful tendency of jurisprudence because it allows historical injustice to be seen as neutral authority, suppresses the particularity of individual cases, and destroys paths for generative and novel advances in the moral learning of jurisprudence.

Stare Decisis Launders Historical Injustice

Stare decisis is more than just a foundational concept in the American legal system enforcing precedent. There are nuances and limits to its application: for example, vertical stare decisis is the idea that the decisions of higher courts take precedence over the decisions of lower courts, and horizontal stare decisis holds that precedent should be held at an inter-appellate level (American Bar Association, 2022). Judges can avoid or overturn precedent based on legal nuance or reasoning, but it is incredibly rare and difficult to do so. What the nuances of stare decisis have in common is clear: precedent is treated as a historical consensus and proxy for correctness. While it can be useful for internal consistency, stare decisis can contain exclusionary dogmatic residue and often fails to capture the current cultural consensus.

For example, Plessy v Ferguson (1896) held that racial segregation was constitutionally permissible as long as the separate facilities provided were nominally equal (Oyez, n.d.). This ruling reflected the dominant racial consensus of its time and became an authoritative force until Brown v. Board of Education overturned it in 1954 (Justia Law, n.d.). Plessy v Ferguson, then, was effectively laundered through the judicial system for almost sixty years before it was struck down. At what point did the cultural consensus change, and how long did Plessy v Ferguson evade the consensus? It can safely be assumed that, at the very least, a large chunk of the population (African Americans) disagreed with this ruling from the beginning. Whites of the time might argue that the 1870 ratification of the 15th Amendment allowed African Americans to vote, so there is no real problem.

Unfortunately, this is yet another case of dogmatic laws slipping through the cracks. In 1870, the 15th Amendment was ratified to say that "the right of citizens of the United States to vote shall not be denied or abridged by the United States or by any State on account of race, color, or previous condition of servitude (U.S. Const. amend. XV, § 1).” While this established that the government could not deny voting based on race, color, or a previous condition of servitude, it did not affirmatively state that it is a fundamental right of all U.S. citizens to vote. The Confederate states used this loophole to deny minorities voting rights based on arbitrary literacy tests, poll taxes, intimidation, and fraud (National Archives Team, 2020). This loophole was not remedied until the Voting Rights Act of 1965 (Crayton, 2023). In this case, a ratification of the Constitution led to historical dogma laundering its way through time for almost 100 years.

These two cases show how one bad law creates a resonance that spirals, infecting and inculcating dogma, and paving the way for biased precedents to be instantiated and fortified by other precedents. These resonant precedents and laws have an outward echoing effect on the population. Since the larger population’s behavior is informed by its limits (laws), and those limits ostensibly allow for both a totalizing segregation and oppression of racial minority’s voting rights, the larger population’s behavior will reflect and pass down those allowances, making cultural, political, and legal change all the more difficult.

Stare Decisis Suppresses the Particularity of Cases

Particularity within cases is one of the most ignored truths of the legal system. This ignorance comes from humanity’s obsession with identity. We tend to focus on generalities and identities, rather than affirming the differences in everything. For example, the generality of an apple will be applied to every apple on the apple tree. Regardless of shape, size, age, color, markings, or position in space, every apple is just identified as an apple. We have long since forgotten how to observe things as ontologically unique, as things different in themselves. Deleuze argues in Difference and Repetition that identity thinking actively suppresses the singularity of reality. The apple becomes merely an instance of the generality of “apple," its particular qualities irrelevant to how it is classified and treated.

“Representation fails to capture the affirmed world of difference. Representation has only a single centre, a unique and receding perspective, and in consequence a false depth (Deleuze, 1968, p. 55).”

The law works in identical representational bounds to the apple. Every case, regardless of its particulars, is placed under a classification and a sub-classification. Then, under common law, the relevantly similar features of the case in question to other cases must be found. Finding relevant similarities allows ideological judgment to sneak in by giving judges the ability to frame a case as (x) vs (y), thus pre-emptively deciding what precedent will affect the ruling. The current controversy surrounding Uber drivers exemplifies this framing problem: a driver logs onto the app, accepts rides, gets paid per trip, and logs off. In court, the judge must decide whether these drivers are employees or independent contractors. If they are categorized as the former, labor law applies; they get minimum wage protections, benefits, unionization rights, etc. If they are deemed the latter, Uber owes them nothing beyond the fare cut. The divisiveness of state rulings reifies the importance of judicial framing: New York, Texas, and Georgia have held that drivers are independent contractors, while California did not (Helping the Hurt Personal Injury Lawyers, n.d.). Additionally, rulings were based on a once again anachronistic conception of employment using the conceived-in-1947 “Economic Reality Test.” This test comprises the following six sufficient but not necessary factors that might deem a worker an employee: opportunity for profit or loss depending on managerial skill, investments by the worker and the employer, permanence of the work relationship, nature and degree of control, whether the work performed is integral to the employer’s business, and skill and initiative. (U.S. Department of Labor, 2024). The ambiguity of this test can be analogized to the previously stated ratification of the 15th Amendment of the Constitution, where ambiguity allows dogma to be laundered in, further illustrating a judge’s ability to choose biasedly. The Uber case exemplifies that the facts do not determine the frame; the frame determines which facts matter. Jerome Frank correctly identifies this reality on page 100 of his book Law and the Modern Mind (1930):

“The process of judging, so the psychologists tell us, seldom begins with a premise from which a conclusion is subsequently worked out. Judging begins rather the other way around - with a conclusion more or less vaguely formed; a man ordinarily starts with such a conclusion and afterwards tries to find premises which will substantiate it. If he cannot, to his satisfaction, find proper arguments to link up his conclusion with premises which he finds acceptable, he will, unless he is arbitrary or mad, reject the conclusion and seek another.”

Once again, there is a clear resonance occurring within all of these moving parts. The dogmatic law is used to inform the dogmatic precedent, which in turn the judge uses to form a dogmatic ruling - and it’s all perfectly legal.

Stare Decisis Destroys Paths for Moral Learning in Law

This resonance creates clear problems for the ideal of jurisprudence. A main feature of a legal system ought to be moral progress, or matching cultural morality with the law. As Ronald Dworkin says on page 413 of his book, Law’s Empire:

“Law’s empire is defined by attitude, not territory or process or organic structure...It is an interpretive, self-reflective attitude addressed to politics in the broadest sense. It is a protestant attitude that makes each citizen responsible for imagining what his society’s public commitments to principle are, and what these commitments require in new circumstances.”

There is no past tense in Dworkin’s language. Law is not a process to be followed, a territory to march over, a historical truth to reify. It is a reflection of the here and now. A legal system committed to moral integrity should be capable of learning, of recognizing that its predecessors were wrong, and of correcting course. Stare decisis directly opposes this capacity.

Entrenching a bad precedent requires just one ruling, and from then on out, it is passively inherited by subsequent rulings. As shown with Plessy v. Ferguson, correcting precedent requires decades of litigation, favorable political conditions, and unbiased judges. Plessy v. Ferguson shackled uncorrupt judges, advocates of racial equality, and dogmatic citizens who went along with the law for more than half a century. And what for? For the sake of consistency?

It is possible that, beyond consistency, the idea of stare decisis is attractive to legal systems because of its age. The system conflates age with wisdom, holding the pastness of a ruling as evidence of its correctness. But a wrong held consistently does not become a right through repetition; it just becomes ideologically camouflaged. In this way, it seems the U.S. legal system’s drive for consistency produces the appearance of justice with a coherent body of law, instead of actual justice, which is achieved through taking each particular case as its own affirmation of justice. Moral learning is best done when systematic abstractions are stripped away, and the present facts - not the frame, the history, the consistency - are made the only true precedent.

Alternatives

One possible way of achieving a present-focused legal system is formulated by Ajume Wingo in his upcoming paper “Law, Power and the Formation of the Juridical Subject.” This essay introduces the concept of an Embodied Associative Jurisprudence comprising five pillars based on the Cameroonian legal system. In Pillar II, Wingo puts forth the idea of collective judicial amnesia.

“This practice does not entail literal forgetfulness but the temporary suspension of memory’s authority during deliberation. Adjudicators remain aware of historical precedent and personal experience, yet they refuse to allow these elements to dictate the outcome of present disputes.”

By utilizing this technique, judges retain their knowledge of legal history, present laws, and personal experience, but remain relationally unbound to those facts. During juridical proceedings, outcomes are solely based on the righteous judgment of the particulars of the case at hand. While this formation is currently in its theoretical stage as to how it would apply to society in the West, occidental countries that use a civil law system and do not utilize stare decisis are well-established and operate across much of continental Europe: France, Germany, Switzerland, Austria, the Netherlands, etc.

Objections

It might seem that consistency in the legal system is necessary because legal stability is vital for political stability. If the law is unstable, then the convicted might believe the system is unjust based on a previous ruling. This is a general problem with the socius - things must be categorized based on a generality, and nothing can be seen as a unique difference in itself. Much can be said about the political and social implications of the West’s heuristics, but for this paper, the most apt response is that reductions based on identity have led to some of the most pernicious outcomes in history (e.g., WWII, slavery). Beyond problems of identity, there is a conflation made between stability as an instrumentally valuable tool to achieve justice and stability as intrinsically valuable in itself. Stable injustice is not better than stable justice, and actual laws can be made clear without precedent.

One could argue that stare decisis prevents present judges from being corrupt, and if judges must conform to precedent, they cannot as readily insert their own beliefs into rulings. However, as has been stated above, judges can still be corrupt through their framing of a case. This objection also ignores the fact that past corruption exists. By arguing that historical precedent hinders present bias, one is just shifting the problem to the biased historical precedent, and to correct all biased rulings in American history, one would have to take a recursive path to the first dogmatic precedent. Moreover, it is good that judges express their attitudes about a given case - that is part of their job. The question is not whether judges should impose value, it’s whether they should be bound to the values of their culturally anachronistic predecessors over their own values.

One may agree with the criticisms put forth in this paper, but be troubled by the seemingly glaring implications. If we abandon stare decisis for its historical dogma, suppression of particularities, and hindrance of moral learning, should we not also abandon all laws, since they are historically instantiated? The answer here is complicated. On one hand, the legal system is deeply rooted in identity and generality by nature. It cannot, in its current form, comprehend the affirmation of difference itself. On the other hand, especially in the U.S., it is an impossible task to do away with all laws. Globalization, checks and balances, intersectionality, and cultural ambiguity make a task like this nearly impossible. To get rid of all laws is to let Atlas shrug, to either degenerate this nation to a primitive state of tribal authority, or to open it up completely, anarchizing the nation and causing a proliferation of injustices to occur.

But laws themselves are not a problem because they were implanted in the past; they are a problem when they box in their own semantic content. Precedent is the puppetmaster that dictates how the law must be interpreted based on how it was interpreted before. Laws that remain semantically ambiguous but are agreed upon by a community (e.g., murder is prima facie wrong) do not contain historical dogmatic residue since their meaning can change depending on cultural consensus. In fact, the existence of laws supports the claim that precedent is harmful, since precedent is evidence that past cultural consensus agreed with the past understanding of a law, and forces the historical definition of that law onto the present community. Precedent, then, acts as an evil cementation of laws already agreed upon. If justice is a principle rooted in righting present inequalities, then it is best to maintain laws, but not to place emphasis on their semantics, and rather allow cultural values circling them to remain authoritative. It is clear that historical precedent merely gets in the way of justice, asserting the past onto the present, and possibly hindering the production of true equality.

Conclusion

“Repetition is not generality. Repetition and generality must be distinguished in several ways...To repeat is to behave in a certain manner, but in relation to something unique or singular, which has no equal or equivalent (Deleuze, 1968, p. 1).”

As Deleuze suggests, the problematic nature of stare decisis lies in the conflation of repetition and generality. Stare decisis defiles both the rulings made off precedent and the precedent itself by reducing them to false, representative, repetitions. It allows historical injustice to be made into a neutral authority, a cancerous proliferation of a reality that necessarily can only happen once (when it happened). It suppresses the particularity of individual cases, reducing them to what was, and it destroys the system’s ability to look forward and take steps towards a better moral future. Stare decisis forces the U.S. legal system to crane its neck backwards, entranced by the past, and pulled away from the reality in front of it. It is prudent that judges do not maintain a consistent gaze behind them, or even look over the present towards the future. They must solely look at what is in front of them and judge the here and now, for that is the only place justice can be found.
