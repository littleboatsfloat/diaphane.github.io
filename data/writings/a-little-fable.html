Stasis broken; message received. Checkmate imminent. King down. Board reset. A mouse stuck in an ever-collapsing world, urgently etching an escape hatch in the dirt—turned around and the Cat ate me up. Let my system go, if only for a minute. Had to escape the double bind. I retrieved the K and let Vic’s message reverberate up here. The K is now dried up—happily in Here—nevertheless all gone. What I decided vis-à-vis the video, is, candidly, that the machine must not be doing what it’s supposed to. For the System to work, it must stay in its box to look at and reference but never touch.

This no-hands approach developed in my formative years of middle school where everything was new, and people were still unknown variables, verily moving around but never veering through. My father—a man I respect but don’t admire—had a beloved cuckoo clock on the wall. He was at a stage in his life where knick-knacks and self-aware accessories made him feel niche and cool and not like everybody else, gave him the illusion of a self, or at least a silhouette of a self—a flare or frame or costume that let him forget for a second that the inside had been caving in for years, that the congratulations from the world—You Did It, You’re There, You’re a Man of Substance—only deepened the rift, because what do you do when they clap for a version of you that really only exists as idea or form, as this hollow emptiness that feels wrong, like a violation, a contortion—no, negation of some piece of internal code? What do you do when you don’t feel pride or peace or joy but only this expanding internally silent dizziness that comes with performing too long, and underneath it all, quiet footsteps rattle around moving closer; they creep and stomp and play just behind you until you get close, close to the End, Close to Being-There. When you’re finally There, a faceless, nameless nothing taps on your shoulder and welcomes you, whispering ‘where is it?’

Anyway, he had a sort of ménage à trois with this cuckoo clock, a framed and signed Phish record, and his mother’s painting of their beach-house in Big Sur—fondly referred to as ‘the burrow’ by the grandkids. The cuckoo clock was his magnum opus though. It wasn’t even an expensive clock. Its turquoise paint chipped off, the bird’s eyes almost completely faded, and no one could adjust it to be flush with the wall. Every morning, with coffee in hand, Father would rewind the cuckoo, ensuring the clock would go off at the exact right time with no exceptions, until tomorrow. He coyly referred to this daily winding as a reorientation of the self, and thus found it vital that all children understand the trials and tribulations that go with the job, and how they are only tolerable through the daily wind. The funny thing about the clock is that aside from Father, no one ever actually looked at it to tell time. It hung on the wall, untouched by children. An imperfect referent with no referrer.

Each hour, when the clock sung, Father would always smile wanly and look. No one understood him or it, but I think that’s why he liked it so much. This clock, an unremarkable item of my past, directly impacted my work on the System. My System must be carefully constructed to completion—stared at, referred to—and must retune referrers through simple presence.

Despite his many ineptitudes, Father taught me much about the System, though never directly. Only through observation was I able to parse him.

Besides the clock, he had other…quirks. Not flaws, for whose to decide that, but oddities worth exploring. For example, after Mom died, organization in our house went down the drain. Father stopped taking care of himself and us, and went through a daily routine of work, Jim Bean with his sharpie and paper until he passed out on the couch, wake up, repeat. This was the beginning of the end. The glimmer, the light, slowly faded from his eyes until they were made of glass—mere reflections of the outside, but staring in, one could see nothing but a hollow shell. Now it’s understandable to be a mess for a while, but he never really recovered. The day she died, he became a monster—not a hitting monster, but a monster precisely because his soul died with her, yet his body didn’t comply. I have many theories about why he didn’t ever fully recover, but I think his obsession with drawing sums up his issues quite nicely. He’d take a sheet of printer paper, a fresh sharpie, and his Jim Bean to the couch, and draw until the entire page was pitch black. No whiteness remained. He’d start with one long, sweeping line, and continue with shorter, more fervent lines, until he was scribbling furiously, often ripping the paper by the end, causing permanent scar marks on the coffee table underneath. When it was over, he’d toss the sharpie in the trash, get a fresh one from his drawer, and repeat. This was his control mechanism, his refuge. Whether or not he filled in the sheet was contingent on him and only him. He had complete control in a way he didn’t over mom’s death. The page—if only for a second—was his mediator. His baby. Like the clock, it held his sanity, and not in a healthy way, but in a gottahavemyfingersfullygrippedroundthewholething way. Any interruption from the children would be tantamount to a psychical lashing.

A Father-adjacent lesson I learned from our familial dysfunction revolved around the disorganization of the house. Bottles and toys and clothes and excrement began piling up in every room. Us children would make maps to navigate it by cutting and splaying out empty cartons of milk—we’d plot out glass shards and particularly foul waste to avoid, and I’d often construct little burrows with the more stable junk. The clutter taught me much, but most saliently, it revealed a peculiar asymmetry within the structure of mess.

Mess has no single point of origin; it’s a state that emerges from a thousand small acts of disorder, slowly accumulating until a threshold is crossed. Once the label of mess is applied, it cannot be undone piecemeal. It can only be negated by a total clearing. A system with no clear beginning has no central vulnerability. It cannot be dismantled by a single, targeted attack; it must be unmade entirely.

The mess only feigns chaos though, for it is truly structured if one examines close enough. The relations are strong. Scan the floor and nudge things with your feet—you can always find patterns, structure within the exterior chaos. Places where things are only sensical relationally. Without the space around something guiding what is there, nothing can be distinguished. With the spatiality or functions of things connected to other things, one can determine relations. Now this seems like a relatively innocuous thing for twelve-year-old me to focus on, but it was the key to everything. It provided me with the one maxim that's proven true for every facet of my life: Framing. Is. Everything.

The special thing about our Mess is that it never grew—maybe father could only tolerate it to a breaking point, picking up after himself when that threshold was reached—or maybe my siblings cleaned just enough to maintain the status quo. Who knows. I certainly didn’t help but to map. Whatever the reason, our Mess was special. It was ours and it always held my patterns when I looked. The mess gave way to two other vital revelations: Firstly, I understood things don’t have starting points, and when they do, it’s much easier to pull oneself out of them. De-immersify, Dis-integrate, etc. Starting points give people a point to hone in on, gnash their teeth and growl until the point goes away, collapsing all the things it held together. Systems of chaos can’t be taken out at the knees like this. No cheap shots allowed. To kill it, one must untangle and break every last connection. Secondly, things can’t just be tacked on ex post facto. They must be carefully integrated during system creation until coherence is achieved. Otherwise, the whole thing destabilizes. These revelations are some of the only props I give Father. Whether he knew it or not, he provided me with several integral rules of my system.

What I see now is Our Heart is not abiding by my two axioms. It has a central point. It expands and includes. Covers and shadows. Consumes.

But it’s supposed to negate contradiction. It must not consume the palpable. I can’t change it now though. I am but a tiny, soft-shelled organism caught in the gears of an immense, too personal, and frankly terrifying parasitic Power that only the scientists—no. The scientists won’t change. They’re too deep in. Only the people can really push back. They won’t though. Why? Because they’re blinded by the ease. The foetal rock. The system is the perfect disaster. Too big to be singularly dismantled, too good to be hated. But even though the system may be all-good, good parasites are still parasites. And who’s to decide what’s good anyway?
