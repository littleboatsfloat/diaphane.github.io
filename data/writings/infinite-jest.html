To celebrate the 30th anniversary of Infinite Jest, a new special edition of the novel was released with a foreword by Michelle Zauner, the acclaimed singer-songwriter and best-selling author. Selecting Zauner quickly became a divisive choice, as many die-hard Wallace fans saw the decision as a marketing gimmick by the publishing house intended to attract a new audience to the book—namely, women. Following the release, people quickly took to the internet to express their dissent, often citing the second line of the foreword, where Zauner says that those who tend to finish the book “are a breed of college-aged men who talk over you, a sect of pedantic, misunderstood young men for whom, over the course of thirty years, Infinite Jest has become a rite of passage.” [1]

It is likely, however, that Zauner prefaced the foreword with these remarks precisely to eschew being used as the marketing tool the publishing house wanted her to be. She goes on to express genuine adulation for the prophecies, mental acuity, and immense grief contained within Infinite Jest.

Despite its reputation, the novel does much more than attract performative males and incels. It brings together recovering addicts, political and literary theorists, people struggling with depression, and those who simply want to embark on a long Odyssean journey through avant-garde film, Québécois terrorists, and an onslaught of lengthy footnotes. These people have something in common: they are frustrated by the overstimulating, scary, and often empty-feeling world that modernity embodies. They want an answer—a sign from the universe that, while the world might feel nihilistic and bleak, there is still a beauty, a kind of moral redemptiveness, that connects us all more than we can ever know.

Beneath all its chaos, Infinite Jest has a very intentional idea behind it. Wallace was trying to send a message not only to writers, but to readers as well: a signal that the gig is up, that literature, increasingly forced to rely on flashy intellect without real substance to stay afloat, risks losing sight of what literature is really about in the first place. But the novel does not merely describe this problem. It makes the reader experience it. [5]

To understand how, it is necessary to step back.

I. The Postmodern Cage

Wallace wanted to transcend the postmodern era into which he was born. Growing up reading the likes of Pynchon, DeLillo, and Gaddis, he was fascinated by their mesmerizing prose and by techniques like irony, metafiction, and moral detachment, which they used to critique the hypocrisies of the fifties and sixties. But Wallace believed that, by the late eighties, this form of fiction had run its course. It was no longer primarily a strategy of critique, but had become a default cultural posture, simply mimicking what had sold before. In a 1993 interview with Larry McCaffery, Wallace stated, “Irony’s useful for debunking illusions, but most of the illusion-debunking in the U.S. has now been done and redone.” [2]

Postmodernism had become empty: a hubless wheel spinning ever faster and ever forward, rolling further and further away from its genesis as a sincere criticism of the society at hand.

But how do you move past the vacuous hole literature has become? How do you break out of the postmodern cage?

For Wallace, the answer requires trying to provide solutions to the problems postmodernism has become so skilled at identifying. But this is risky. In the same interview with McCaffery, Wallace states, “Few artists dare to try to talk about ways of working toward redeeming what’s wrong, because they’ll look sentimental and naïve to all the weary ironists. Irony’s gone from liberating to enslaving.” When sales and literary legitimacy are predicated on good reviews from critics and academics, escaping the cage begins to seem like an impossible endeavor. [2]

II. Between the Head and the Heart

Wallace’s first major attempt to escape it came in the novella “Westward the Course of Empire Takes Its Way,” a critique of John Barth’s hyper-metafictional short story “Lost in the Funhouse.” Westward’s title alludes to Emanuel Leutze’s mural Westward the Course of Empire Takes Its Way, displayed in the west stairway of the House wing of the U.S. Capitol. The painting suggests a kind of divine right to hegemonic expansion, in which America, initially formed in the East, must march westward and conquer. For Wallace, however, this expansion becomes a metaphor for the postmodern strategies of his predecessors, who have created a literary empire that has conquered its territory so thoroughly that it has reached a dead end, collapsing into total self-reference. [3]

“Lost in the Funhouse” tells the story of a boy named Ambrose who travels to Ocean City and gets lost in a literal funhouse at an amusement park. At one point, Barth writes, “In a funhouse mirror-room you can’t see yourself go on forever, because no matter how you stand, your head gets in the way.” The line becomes a metaphor for the problem Wallace sees in literature: your own intellect will always get in the way of true reflection. Wallace wants his writing to genuinely affect the reader, but language—and both the reader’s and writer’s heads—stand in the way.

So Wallace sets out to solve this conundrum in Westward. He directly satirizes Barth’s story by bringing it to its metafictional and postmodern extreme, concocting bizarre characters and plotlines that eventually converge at a reunion for actors from McDonald’s commercials, where, inevitably, there will be a Ronald McDonald funhouse.

The main character is Mark Nechtr, a professional archer who carries his bow everywhere and serves as a clear stand-in for Wallace himself. During a chaotic airport scene, Mark explains how an archer must account for the fact that an arrow does not fly perfectly true. To hit the bullseye, the archer must aim slightly off-center to compensate for unknown forces acting upon the arrow in flight. [3]

Wallace is making an argument about fiction. Because the postmodern reader’s cynical defenses make a direct shot at the heart impossible, the writer must employ techniques like metafiction and irony to bypass those defenses. The writer must aim somewhere between the head and the heart. By the end of Westward, Wallace’s project is revealed not as a rejection of metafictional tools, but as an argument for a more sophisticated use of them: one capable of finally “hitting the reader in the heart” and restoring some sense of love to a literary landscape that had become cold and mechanical.

In D. T. Max’s biography of Wallace, he quotes Wallace calling Westward his “breakthrough” and saying that he had “said all he needed to say” in it. At the time, Wallace clearly believed deeply in the story. Yet, ironically, his metafiction had gone too far. Reviews were lackluster, and the brilliance Wallace believed he had achieved did not catch the eyes he hoped it would. [4]

Importantly, “Westward” was written around the same time Wallace began thinking about Infinite Jest. A 1986 Yaddo application lists Infinite Jest as the project he intended to work on while there. Yaddo is a prestigious artists’ residency in upstate New York where writers and artists—including figures like Sylvia Plath, James Baldwin, and Cormac McCarthy—have been given time and space to focus on their creative work. Since “Westward” was published in 1989, its failure was likely still fresh in Wallace’s mind as he seriously began constructing Infinite Jest.

III. Taking Better Aim

This context is important because Infinite Jest can be read as Wallace’s next attempt at solving the problem that “Westward” had identified but failed to overcome.

What follows is, of course, only one reading of the novel—an attempt to capture Wallace’s conception of its meta-theme. Everything in Infinite Jest is pluralistic in meaning, and readers will inevitably take different things from it. While the desire to transcend postmodernism may have been one catalyst for the book, Wallace was almost certainly writing its characters, images, and structures with a multiplicity of meanings in mind. Ultimately, what one takes away from the novel remains up to the reader.

When Infinite Jest first reached the hands of its editor, Michael Pietsch, it had a remarkably different structure: that of a Sierpinski gasket, a triangle that infinitely fractalizes in on itself. One of the most notable features of this earlier form is that it had a different first chapter. Its opening was what is now the novel’s third chapter, “1 April — Year of the Tucks Medicated Pad,” while the published opening—Hal’s famous breakdown during his college interview—appeared much later.

In this original opening chapter, Hal meets with a professional conversationalist who, it becomes increasingly obvious, is actually his father, James Incandenza, in disguise. During the conversation, Hal mentions that his father seems to suffer from the hallucination that Hal never speaks. James, in turn, desperately attempts to get Hal to speak genuinely to him. Yet father and son simply quip back and forth, never really engaging with one another so much as talking around and over each other. The dialogue ends with James asking for “just one conversation that does not end in terror. That does not end like all the others, with Hal staring and James swallowing.”

This scene matters because it is the only one in which James directly speaks with Hal. Their conversation reveals the central failure of their relationship. Hal can define words, provide synonyms, and give the etymology of half the dictionary, but he cannot have a sincere conversation with his father. [5]

IV. Hal Is Literature

Hal’s behavior bears an eerie resemblance to Wallace’s critique of postmodernism.

Returning, then, to the breakdown scene that opens the published novel—and which is presumably the final Hal chapter chronologically—we find a remarkably different human being. After being beaten down by withdrawal, fascistic academia, and presumably the Entertainment, Hal has changed. He can no longer cope by overintellectualizing his problems. He has begun to experience the collapse of the structure of his life emotionally, in a way that he never could before.

Internally, Hal insists that he is not merely a machine: he feels, believes, has opinions, and desperately wants the people around him to understand that he cares. Yet when he attempts to communicate all of this, the people across the table perceive only horror. What is perfectly articulate inside Hal arrives outside him as something grotesque and unintelligible. [5]

Hal has gone from an apparently unfeeling intellectual prodigy, versed in irony and articulation, to a deeply feeling, opinionated boy who has lost the ability to express his intellect. This loss of articulation is not instantaneous. It develops slowly throughout the novel. We see moments in which Hal simply cannot recite what he once could. He tries to speak, and words fail him.

The tragedy is that Hal knows exactly what has gone wrong: “I’d tell you all you want and more, if the sounds I made could be what you hear.” His interior life has not disappeared. The connection between interiority and communication has. [5]

And the people around him confirm the severity of that rupture. While Hal experiences himself as lucid and articulate, those listening hear only indecipherable, animalistic noises. What Hal means and what the world receives have become almost completely severed from one another. [5]

Hal becomes trapped in a paradox: to express feeling, he must get beyond his intellect; but to express his intellect, he seems compelled to suppress feeling.

As Wallace would later write in The Pale King: “How odd I can have all this inside me and to you it’s just words.” [6]

Except with Hal, the problem has become even worse. It is no longer that everything inside him gets reduced to words. The words themselves can no longer get out.

Wallace spends Infinite Jest trying to turn postmodernism against itself, only to end with grotesque silence. Hal can therefore be read as literature itself—or, at the very least, as an embodiment of David Foster Wallace’s personal project for literature. He begins as everything Wallace distrusted about postmodern fiction: astonishingly intelligent, endlessly articulate, capable of dazzling displays of knowledge, and yet somehow incapable of communicating what matters most. By the time he learns how intensely he feels, the mechanism through which those feelings might reach another person has broken down.

V. The Jest Is Infinite

If Hal is literature, then his father, James Orin Incandenza, becomes something like David Foster Wallace.

James is literally referred to as “Himself,” and even after his death he remains present in wraith form, moving furniture, scanning Don Gately’s brain, and putting DMZ on toothbrushes. He is always there, manipulating people and objects alike. “Do not underestimate objects,” Lyle says.

James is an artist obsessed with pushing the limits of what art itself can be. Yet this is not enough. Critics love his work for all the wrong reasons, while his protégé, Hal, has been commodified and mechanized. His wife is secretly involved with the Québécois separatists. His eldest son, Orin, is a kind of postmodern nightmare: commercialized, oedipalized, paranoid, and egoistic. His youngest son is not even biologically his.

So what can James do?

He can put down the bottle and show the world the logical conclusion of its consumption.

And is that not, in some sense, exactly what Wallace did? He sought help, went to AA, and returned to the ideas of “Westward” with a new understanding of what they required. James creates the samizdat and kills himself. He leaves behind a piece of art so intoxicating that it becomes something like a virus to be contracted. He leaves a copy for Hal, knowing that Hal will need it to continue the cycle of literature. He needs Hal to understand that what he is becoming—and what the world around him has become—threatens to replace what is sacred about literature altogether.

The artist sacrifices himself for his art form, leaving behind a pleasure so total that it threatens to annihilate not only the world, but the desire to have a world outside pure entertainment at all.

It is a humanist’s nightmare and a solipsist’s dream.

The Entertainment, then, becomes an oedipalized depiction of the pendulum of literature itself. It shows us what we really want, what we truly desire: a caring and totalizing mother who takes care of our wants and needs before we can even formulate them ourselves. It cuts thought out of the picture completely. It desires for us, without us, and consummates that desire without a single synapse needing to be fired.

And so the jest is infinite because the cycle is inescapable and never-ending.

Literature, in order to stay alive, must become ever more entertaining. But in doing so, it risks becoming only entertainment, thereby ceasing to be literature at all. It is a forever-unstable pendulum between the intellect and the heart, and all the writer can do is aim the bow somewhere between the two and pray that the arrow lands true.
